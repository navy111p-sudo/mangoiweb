1 Why this makes video freeze but not voice

The internet has two ways to send things. Think of them as courier and registered mail.

UDP1 — the courier
If a package is late, it is thrown away and the next one goes through. Video looks a little rougher for a second, then continues. This is what video calls are built for.
TCP2 — registered mail
Every package must arrive, in order. If one is late, everything behind it waits in line. Video stops dead, then jumps. This is what we are stuck on now.

Voice survives registered mail because it is tiny — about 30 kbps, and the browser can paper over small gaps. Video is roughly 40× bigger. One late packet stalls the whole picture, then a burst arrives. That is exactly the “freeze… jump… freeze” teachers describe.

So the problem is not your internet speed. We measured round-trip times7 of 113–324 ms during the broken calls — that is a perfectly normal, healthy connection. The traffic is simply being forced down the wrong lane.

3 What we already fixed on our side

We are not asking you to carry this alone. Since 3 September we shipped:

  • Stopped the flicker. When the line got bad, our software switched to audio-only and then tried to come back after only 8 seconds — over and over. That is why the picture kept appearing and vanishing. It now waits 32 seconds of a genuinely stable line before restoring video.
  • Stopped teachers' laptops recording. Every browser in the room was making its own recording, which used the same upload and the same CPU as the live video. Teachers no longer record automatically.
  • Lower start on slow lines. Calls now begin at a lower quality when the line is known to be slow, instead of flooding it and then backing off.
  • We can now see the route. Every call records whether it went direct or through a relay, and over UDP or TCP. That is how we found this — and it is how we will confirm your fix worked.
  • A backup path (server-relayed video, an SFU11). Built and ready, switched off. It helps on connections that can never be opened up — but it is not a substitute for this fix. It is also WebRTC, so where UDP is blocked it falls back to TCP as well.

📖 Glossary — the technical words, in plain English

Follow the small orange numbers from the text above. You do not need all of these — 1, 2 and 4 are enough to make the request.

1 · UDP
The “throw it away if it’s late” way of sending data. Video calls and online games use it, because being on time matters more than being complete. A little roughness is fine; stopping is not. This is the lane that is currently blocked.
2 · TCP
The “everything arrives, in order” way. Web pages and downloads use it, because being complete matters more than being fast. It is the worst possible choice for live video: one late packet and everything behind it waits.
3 · WebRTC
The standard technology browsers use to send live video and audio to each other. Our classroom is built on it; Zoom and Google Meet use the same family. It is designed around UDP.
4 · Firewall · outbound vs inbound
A firewall is the gatekeeper that decides which traffic may pass (usually built into the router). Outbound is traffic leaving your office; inbound is traffic coming in from the internet. ✅ We are only asking about outbound, so this does not weaken your security — nothing new becomes reachable from outside.
5 · TURN relay
A server that forwards video on your behalf when two people cannot reach each other directly. It is meant to be an emergency exit used occasionally. Right now 100% of calls are going through it.
6 · STUN
A small “what does my address look like from outside?” lookup server. A direct connection cannot even be attempted without asking it first — and it also uses UDP. So when UDP is blocked, direct connections never even get started.
7 · Round-trip time (RTT)
How long a signal takes to go and come back. Korea ↔ Philippines is normally 100–300 ms. We measured 113–324 ms during the broken calls — completely normal, which is why “the line is too slow” does not explain what teachers are seeing.
8 · CGNAT
When the internet provider makes many customers share one internet address. It saves addresses, but it means nobody outside can pinpoint you — which makes direct connections much harder. A plan with a public IP removes it.
9 · Direct connection (peer to peer)
The teacher’s computer and the student’s computer talking straight to each other, with no server in between. Fastest and best. We currently have 0 out of 46.
10 · Port
If an internet address is a building, a port is the room number. UDP 3478 means “please let deliveries reach room 3478 in that building.” The three lines in the request are the rooms our classroom uses.
11 · SFU (server-relayed video)
Everyone sends to a nearby server, which forwards it on — the structure Zoom and Google Meet use. We have built one and left it switched off. ⚠ It is not a replacement for this request — it is also WebRTC, so where UDP is blocked it falls back to TCP in exactly the same way.
12 · Packet
One small piece of data. A single second of video is split into hundreds of them. “Packets are being dropped” means those pieces are disappearing on the way.
