1. Speaking is now in all 21 learning games ★

The CEO's instruction was clear — every game must require speaking, and above all speaking in full sentences. And the sentence the student speaks must come back to be heard and repeated — a virtuous cycle.

★ I first opened all 21 games one by one to see how each actually works. The result was unexpected — only 11 games genuinely required speaking. Six were half-done (a button the student could ignore and still move on, loose grading, or no record kept), and four had no speaking at all — one of them being "Space Monster Hunt", the flagship card pinned to the top of the list.
★ I built a "4-step speaking cycle" and put it into all 21 games. ① The meaning is hidden and the student hears sound only, twice → ② they say the key word (short, so success comes easily) → ③ they say the whole sentence (if wrong, the model sentence is played again for one more try) → ④ they hear the native voice and repeat it immediately. (This follows the research table on the parent-facing "Why these games work" page — guessing first opens the ears, speaking is what turns knowledge into ability, and spaced re-encounters make it last.)

2. Fixed grading that was far too loose ★

★ Some games were only counting how many target words appeared anywhere in what was heard. So for "I have a red apple", saying "apple red a have I" — the word order completely reversed — still passed, and saying "banana" instead of "apple" also passed. The game literally named "Speaking Quiz" had the loosest grading of all.

Neither passes any more. And we no longer just say "wrong" — the student is told, in one line, exactly what to change. (e.g. saying rice like lice → "keep the tongue tip off the roof of the mouth for r") If a child does not know what to change, they simply repeat the same thing again.

3. The virtuous cycle — yesterday's stuck sentence returns tomorrow, in a different game ★

★ The most important finding was that there was nothing to build. The server endpoint that receives pronunciation scores already existed — but only 1 of the 21 games was using it. The rest scored the student and sent that score nowhere. So we did not build a new server — we only connected the wiring. (Because nothing new was built, server cost does not go up at all.)

Once connected, this is what happens — a sentence the student got stuck on yesterday on a tablet comes back today on their phone, in a different game. Previously the record lived only on that one device and vanished entirely when the student switched devices. Teachers can now also see whether each student's pronunciation is improving.

4. Speaking is required, but not forced

Some students are in a library or on a bus, have no microphone, or opened the page inside KakaoTalk where the microphone is unavailable. So after 3 seconds a "I can't speak out loud right now" button appears. However, skipping earns no points, and that sentence is queued to come back later.

★ One thing we nearly missed — if you do not distinguish a child who could not use the microphone at all from a child who spoke and got it wrong, the child without a microphone is shown "Pronunciation score: 0". We separated the two so that no score is stated in that case.

5. What we deliberately did not touch

The shooting game's speaking mission was left as it is. On inspection it already had a 20-second allowance, star ratings, and playback of the student's own voice. Replacing that to make it match the other games would have made it worse. The only thing missing was sending the record to the server, so that is all we added. (What works for the child comes before making everything uniform.)

"Suspect Mystery" and the escape games also kept their original pass criteria. Their task is not "repeat this sentence exactly" but "explain who the culprit is in a way that makes sense".

6. An automatic check so this wiring cannot break silently ★

★ This kind of failure cannot be caught by eye. The screen keeps working and scores still appear — only the record goes missing. In fact, until this work, 15 games had never sent a single record and nobody knew. So we built a check that blocks the release automatically. (We deliberately broke the wiring to confirm the check actually catches it.)

8. "The Chinese characters all look different" — it was the font ★

★ As the CEO pointed out, Chinese characters were rendering differently from one another. Without a font specified for Chinese characters, a Korean PC draws them with a Korean font (a visibly different letterform), and any character missing from that font is substituted from a different font per character, so even stroke weight varies. I applied a Chinese font only to the Chinese-character and pinyin tone-mark ranges.

(It only uses fonts already installed on the PC, so 0 bytes are downloaded, and Korean and Latin text are untouched.)

14. Compared against competitors — added the 3 features we were missing ★

I checked Mangoi one by one against the 16 features that Korean academy-management products and overseas tutoring-management products all share. 11 were already here. Only 5 were genuinely missing, and I built the 3 that could be done right away.

★ ① Teacher utilization — the share of a teacher's open hours that are actually filled with classes. The real cost of online classes is teacher time, and without this number there was no basis for deciding whether to hire more teachers or fewer. Teachers under 30% are listed separately.
★ ② Blocking overlapping classes — a check existed, but it only caught identical start times. So if a 9:00 class (50 min) existed, booking 9:30 went straight through even though 20 minutes overlap — and nothing stopped the same teacher from already having another student at that time. (That means the teacher has to be in two classrooms at once — it turns into an incident on the day.) It now accounts for class length and checks by teacher as well. Group classes, where several students share one slot on purpose, are not blocked.
★ ③ Waitlist — until now, if the requested time was full the enquiry simply ended there. Now they join a queue, and if a preferred weekday and time are entered we immediately show which teachers are free at that slot. (These are people who already showed interest — far cheaper to win than a brand-new lead.)

The remaining 2 are e-signature contracts and automatic tax-invoice issuing. Both need an external provider contract first, so I left them alone this time.

17. Smoke cut off abruptly when a tank blew up ★

The CEO's report — "when the tank explodes and dies, the fire and smoke get cut off."

★ The cause was unexpected. The part that keeps the wreck billowing smoke sat in a place that only runs while the game is running. But when your own tank is destroyed, the game stops. So the one moment that should be the most dramatic — being destroyed — was the only moment with no smoke at all. All that remained was the one-off flash of the explosion, so it cut out after 2.5 seconds.

Now the wreck keeps smoking and then fades away naturally. Measured:

★ Amount of smoke on screen right after being destroyed (counted every 0.5 s)
Before — 10 · 10 · 8 · 4 · 0  →  gone at 2.5 s (new smoke emitted after the kill: 0)
After   — 16 · 18 · 18 · 9 · 4 · 2 · 0  →  fades naturally at 3.5 s (new smoke emitted after the kill: 14)

The smoke is also a little denser now. (Turning it up all the way makes a black blob that hides the tank, so we stopped partway.)

★ Worth recording that my first diagnosis was wrong. I judged it as "the smoke image is being clipped by the ground" and fixed that — but counting the pixels actually drawn on screen showed a difference of 0.1%. That was not the cause. I reverted that change entirely. Changing draw order without evidence creates new problems where smoke overlaps trees and terrain in front of it.

(Screen problems have several plausible-looking causes, so checking with numbers before fixing turns out to be faster.)

After shipping, I measured again on the live address and confirmed no overlap on either screen.

One thing to note — with the translate button removed, these screens can no longer switch between Korean and English. They follow whatever language was chosen on another screen.
