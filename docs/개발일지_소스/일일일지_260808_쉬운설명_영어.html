Mangoi Daily Log — Saturday, 8 August 2026

Written in plain language · readable by a 15-year-old · midnight to 5 a.m.

Today in one sentence — Our English pronunciation practice was giving high scores even when you mumbled. Digging in, the scoring maths was not wrong — the measuring tool was never measuring pronunciation at all. So we replaced the tool.

1. What was wrong

Mangoi has a feature called "Voice Coach." A student reads an English sentence out loud, an AI listens, and it gives a score.

But if you deliberately mumbled, the score stayed high. A student would then think "I'm doing fine" and move on. It is a practice tool where no practice happens.

First thing we did We did not guess. We recorded the same sentence 20 times and measured the numbers directly — clear readings and mumbled readings, side by side.

2. The real cause — measuring temperature with a tape measure

Here is how the score was built. When a student speaks, a dictation AI turns the sound into text. That AI also reports "how confident I am in what I wrote." We were using that confidence number as the pronunciation score.

There is a trap in this. A dictation AI is trained to guess from context — the same way you understand a friend even when you only half hear them.

What the measurements showed
Sentence readClearlyMumbledGap
Thank you very much for your help.-0.29-0.37almost none
I love studying English with Mangoi.-0.44-0.82wide open

"Thank you very much for your help" is one of the most common sentences in English. The dictation AI gets it right almost without listening — so it stays confident even when you mumble.
"Mangoi" is a word that AI does not know, so it cannot guess. It has to actually listen, and mumbling showed up immediately.

So what we were measuring was not "how well you pronounce" but "how common the sentence is." We were holding a tape measure and trying to read a temperature. No amount of re-marking the scale gives you degrees.

3. How we fixed it — we changed the tool

We needed something that measures the sound phoneme by phoneme. A phoneme is the smallest unit of speech sound. studying, for example, is made of small sound pieces — and this checks whether each piece was actually produced. Context cannot paper over that.

  1. The billing shape fits us. One sentence takes our students 3–7 seconds. The others bill in 15-second blocks — say five seconds, pay for fifteen. You throw away 3×.
  2. Chinese is supported. Mangoi already runs a Chinese voice coach; Speechace is English-only.
  3. There is a server in Korea. Student voices never leave the country — these are minors.

5. Five walls on the way in

Connecting the new tool did not go smoothly. We hit five walls, and each time we left a note explaining why.

  1. Only 2 characters of the password went in. The paste failed and nothing on screen said so. Worse, Azure answers this with a refusal carrying no explanation rather than "wrong password." → It now says "the key is too short" straight away.
  2. Calling two things at once broke it. To be fast we ran "dictation" and "scoring" together, and it failed erratically. → We run them in order. Half a second slower, and reliable.
  3. Azure does not accept the browser's recording format. Sent as-is, the scoring is silently skipped with no sign on screen. → The same microphone now also produces the format Azure accepts.
  4. Azure's simple endpoint ignored the scoring request entirely. It understood the sentence perfectly and returned everything except the score. → We moved to running the official toolkit inside the student's browser.
  5. Every failure was silent. When it didn't work, no reason was recorded anywhere, so all you can do is guess. → Every failure now records why. That single change is what made the last two problems quick to find.
What this taught us The worst state is "it doesn't work and nobody knows why." Spending a little time making failures explain themselves pays for itself almost immediately.
